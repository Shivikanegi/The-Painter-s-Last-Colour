The Painter's Last Colour

You don't hand a spark back to the fire; you use it to light another lantern.

— Story Cover — Generated with ChatGPT

Kenji was never meant to be a samurai. He had wanted to paint. He wanted the heavy mountains, the cherry blossoms, the way the fading light fell softly through the shrine gates at dusk. But a heavy sword was pressed into his waiting hands, and the whole wide world agreed that this was right. He was too obedient to argue. He became the finest swordsman in the province. He became, in the quiet, the loneliest man of all.

His master, Ryuken, found him painting by the lamplight after battle. Just the cherry tree. Nothing else. There were no warriors, there was no victory. Ryuken sat beside him on the ground and whispered softly, "This is the truest thing that I have ever seen you make." Three years went by, and Ryuken slipped away. And Kenji never touched a brush again.

He was sitting by the ruined shrine when the boy appeared. He wasn't hiding. He walked straight up and sat right down, uninvited and bold, the way that only children and the very wise will do. Ink stained his fingers. A badly folded paper bulged within his hands. He opened it up and held it out, without a single word.

It was a painting of the cherry tree. It was rough, it was unpracticed, but it was unmistakably this tree, this silent hill, this ancient shrine. And standing right beneath the branches, a lonely figure in his blackened armor.

"I painted you," the little boy said. "You always look so sad. I thought that maybe this would help."

Kenji stared down at the painting. A single, quiet petal fell, landing perfectly upon the paper. Gently. As if it had been placed by invisible hands. As if an old, familiar teacher was reaching through the veil of living, setting it down and saying: See. The truest thing.

Something heavy unlocked in Kenji’s chest.

"Do you have more paper?" he asked into the quiet.

The boy grinned wide and pulled a crumpled stack from his kimono.

They sat beneath the cherry tree until the stars began to show. The old tired samurai, the small quiet boy. Passing the dark ink between them, painting the mountain, the shrine, the falling petals. Not speaking much. Not needing to. And somewhere in the space between the lamplight and the dark, a calm and luminous presence watched them both. Not with a heavy sadness, but with something closest to the word finally.

Down below, the village saw the light upon the hill, and thought that Kenji kept his lonely vigil. They were not entirely wrong. But for the first time in three long years, the light was not of grief.

It was just two people. Making something beautiful, in the dark.
